Housing

How a housing project actually gets built

Every project in this tracker moves through the same five stages, no matter which city: Proposed (an early filing, sometimes just a preliminary application), Under Review (staff and commissions checking it against zoning, environmental rules, and the general plan), Approved (a Planning Commission or City Council vote), Under Construction, and Completed. A project can sit in "Under Review" for years, or move in months, depending on its complexity and how much community input it draws.

Why so many projects mention "SB330," "Builder's Remedy," or "SB35"

California passed a series of state laws over the last decade specifically to speed up housing approval when cities aren't producing enough of it. SB330 (the Housing Crisis Act of 2019) limits how much a city can downzone or delay housing once an application is filed. SB35 lets qualifying affordable projects skip most discretionary review entirely if a city hasn't met its state-mandated housing targets. The Builder's Remedy is the most aggressive tool: if a city's Housing Element (its state-mandated housing plan) is found legally non-compliant, developers can propose projects that ignore local zoning almost entirely, as long a share of the units are affordable. Cupertino's wave of Builder's Remedy filings in this tracker exists because its Housing Element was found non-compliant for a period.

What "BMR" and affordability numbers actually mean

BMR stands for Below Market Rate - units legally restricted to rent or sell at a set fraction of the area's median income, rather than whatever the market would otherwise charge. Cities usually require a percentage of units in larger projects to be BMR, though the exact percentage and income tiers vary by city and by which state law a project is using. A "100% affordable" project has no market-rate units at all, usually built by a nonprofit or public housing authority rather than a private developer.

Where to actually weigh in

Housing projects are decided by each city's Planning Commission first, with City Council as the final word on appeals. See the Get Involved section below for exact meeting schedules and how to submit public comment on a specific project.

Developments

Commercial, industrial, office, hotel, and retail projects - separate from housing.

What counts as a "development" here, versus housing

This page covers everything that isn't primarily housing: office and R&D campuses, hotels, retail centers, industrial and storage buildings, and mixed-use projects where the commercial portion is the main story. Mixed-use projects with a significant residential component are tracked under Housing instead - a project can appear in both places if it genuinely has both.

Why office/R&D projects matter to the housing conversation

The South Bay's jobs-to-housing imbalance is a direct driver of the housing pressure tracked elsewhere on this site. Every new office or R&D campus approved without matching new housing adds workers who need somewhere to live, in cities that are already behind on state housing targets. Some cities now require a "jobs-housing linkage fee" or a Development Agreement tying commercial approvals to public benefits - watch for these terms in a project's notes.

CEQA and why some projects cite a "Notice of Exemption"

Any discretionary project in California must clear the California Environmental Quality Act (CEQA), either through a full Environmental Impact Report (EIR) or by qualifying for an exemption. Infill projects on already-developed sites within city limits often qualify for a "Class 32" categorical exemption, which is why you'll see "Notice of Exemption" instead of a multi-year EIR process on several projects in this tracker - it's a legitimate, common path, not a shortcut around review.

Development Agreements

For large projects, cities sometimes negotiate a formal Development Agreement - a contract that locks in the project's entitlements for a set number of years (often 20-30) in exchange for public benefits like parkland, infrastructure funding, or affordable housing. Google's Middlefield Park and North Bayshore master plans in Mountain View are both governed by long-term Development Agreements.

Transportation

The kinds of projects tracked here

Transportation covers road and highway changes, Caltrain and light rail infrastructure, bike and pedestrian improvements, and grade separations (where a road or path is rerouted above or below train tracks to remove a dangerous crossing). Unlike housing, most of these are public works projects run directly by the city or a regional agency, not private developers.

Why "VMT" replaced "LOS" in project reviews

Until 2020, California cities measured a project's traffic impact using LOS (Level of Service) - essentially, how much a project slowed down car traffic at nearby intersections. State law (SB743) replaced this with VMT (Vehicle Miles Traveled) - how many total miles of driving a project generates. The shift matters: LOS penalized dense, walkable, transit-served projects for "causing congestion," while VMT rewards them for reducing how much people have to drive. A project near a Caltrain station now often has an easier environmental review specifically because of this change.

Grade separations: why they cost hundreds of millions

Every at-grade Caltrain crossing (where a road crosses the tracks directly, with gates and lights) is a safety and traffic bottleneck as Caltrain moves toward faster, more frequent electrified service. Grade separation projects - lowering the road, raising the tracks, or building an under/overpass - routinely cost $200-700 million per crossing and take a decade or more from first study to completion, which is why projects like Sunnyvale's Caltrain Grade Separation move so slowly compared to a typical development.

Vision Zero

Many cities in this tracker have adopted Vision Zero - a policy goal of eliminating all traffic deaths and severe injuries by a target year, usually through street redesigns (protected bike lanes, shorter crossings, slower design speeds) rather than relying on driver behavior alone. Bicycle and pedestrian projects tracked here are often justified directly by Vision Zero commitments.

About This Dashboard

250 Hamilton Ave–style civic offices exist in every city shown - this tool is independent of all of them.

A working prototype built from real, current public planning records for three South Bay cities. Each city's dataset is a representative sample (not the complete active list) pulled from that city's own development reports, project pages, and boards/commissions pages. Some records are flagged where a status date is old, a permit expired, or a detail wasn't specified in the public record - that's surfaced, not hidden.
